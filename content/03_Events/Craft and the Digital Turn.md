# Craft and the Digital Turn

**Project wrap‑up meeting notes**  
OCAD University, Toronto  
Visiting researchers & practitioners from UK, Tasmania, Mexico, and Canada

---

## 1. Project Overview & Framing

- Central question: **How do digital tools enter craft systems critically—not as neutral add‑ons, but as fundamentally different modes of making?** (Dorie)
    
- Digital tools are _not just tools_: they reshape **process, aesthetics, pedagogy, authorship, access, and labor**.
    
- Origins of the project:
    
    - Early video projects examining _how makers actually learn digital tools_.
        
    - Most participants were **self‑taught**, learning on their own time and budgets, then translating that knowledge into teaching.
        
- Project growth:
    
    - Local C‑grant → national → international research network.
        
    - Strong attention to **connections across geography and time**, and to **student experience**, especially under COVID.
        

---

## 2. Core Research Themes (Across All Sites)

### Process

- Process _shapes_ aesthetic outcomes (David Grimshaw).
    
- Craft is defined less by outcome than by **methodology, material dialogue, and iterative decision‑making**.
    
- Digital tools change the _tempo_ of making (fast iteration vs slow material learning).
    

### Access

- Persistent barriers to tools—especially outside institutions.
    
- Reduced public funding for craft.
    
- COVID accelerated:
    
    - Self‑teaching
        
    - Online tutorials
        
    - Informal peer networks
        
- **Technicians and experts** are crucial—but often overstretched or structurally constrained.
    

### Pedagogy

- Teaching is part of many makers’ own practice.
    
- Online teaching flattened learning:
    
    - Loss of embodied instruction ("literally holding someone’s hands").
        
- Tension between curriculum‑driven instruction and exploratory, risky, playful making.
    

### Digitality

- Digitality understood as:
    
    - Networks
        
    - Processes
        
    - Distributed knowledge
        
- Digital aesthetics are emerging _from_ process, not imposed stylistically.
    

### Craft Values Under Pressure

- Care, repair, patience, authorship, locality, and human trace.

## 3. Site‑Specific Findings

## A. Montreal Interviews (5 practitioners)

**Method**

- Conducted in personal spaces.
    
- In participants’ own languages.
    

**Participants & Practices**

- **Beatrice Dubreuil** — Weaver/designer; hyper‑local materials.
    
- **Hayley Mortin** — Machine knitting, hacking, UX/UI.
    
- **Xenia Lucie Laffely (Concordia)** — Photo editing, long‑arm quilting; reliance on wood/metal; _no digital loom access in Montreal_.
    
- **Sandy Lamb** — Natural dye, large‑scale tapestry; residency‑based work.
    
- **ShopTalk** — Artist‑led educational space prioritizing access to tools.
    

**Key Insights**

- Digital learning paths:
    
    - Mostly self‑taught
        
    - Online tutorials + community knowledge as stepping stones
        
- Outsourcing:
    
    - Textile printing companies used
        
    - Loss of transparency and quality
        
- Toolkits:
    
    - Photoshop, Instagram as core tools
        
- AI:
    
    - Mostly not used personally
        
    - Accepted as collaborator by others
        
    - Concerns: hidden human labor, environmental cost, authorship
        
- Desire to learn more (3D modeling, Jacquard) is **contingent on access**, not urgency.
    

**Notable Quote**

> “Our hands are machines… design comes from a human mind.” (Beatrice)

**Context**

- Small‑scale, speculative practices
    
- Strong emphasis on sustainability and locality
    

**Participants**

- Kate Bowman — Ceramics (AI glaze testing; unsatisfactory results)
    
- Donald Fortescue — Collector of experience
    
- Sarah — Jewelry
    
- Matt — 3D printing
    
- Luke Dineen — Digital fabrication
    
- Murray Antill — Digital fabrication
    
- Bryony Anderson — Material‑led practice, Mount Wellington
    

**Affordances of Digital Tools**

- Smooth hybrid workflows (hand + digital)
    
- Expansion into new materials
    
- Hacking printers and processes
    
- Full‑scale prototyping before fabrication
    
- Faster iteration, personalization
    
- AI as _studio assistant_, not author
    

**Challenges & Losses**

- Loss of material feel and messiness
    
- Homogenization and generic aesthetics
    
- Plastic proliferation, gimmickry
    
- Decline of repair skills
    
- Authorship ambiguity
    

**Why Use Digital**

- To do what hands can’t
    
- To save time for creative decisions
    
- To overcome constraints
    
- Sustainability goals
    
- Economic viability
    

**Reshaping Methodologies**

- Hybrid is now the norm
    
- Low‑fi first approaches
    
- AR for collective decision‑making
    
- AI as co‑pilot
    
- Distributed studios and galleries
    

**Bryony Anderson**

- Material‑led briefs for specific purposes
    
- Resource stewardship (95% selvage use)
    
- Collaborative digital boards
    
- Roughness as a sign of life and care

## 4. Language of Process & Expanded Craft

- Craft vs design vs engineering: distinctions lie in **process, intent, and material dialogue**.
    
- Digital tools act as a “third eye” (e.g., Shine Can, motion capture, VR drawing).
    
- Craft today spans:
    
    - 3D printing & slip‑casting
        
    - Biomaterials
        
    - VR, NFTs
        
- Digital craft still requires **tactile knowledge** to translate screen → object.
    
- New makers are digitally fluent but need embodied making for depth.
    

**Key Positions**

- Avoid fetishizing “digital” as separate—_everything is a hand tool_.
    
- AI does not think; it assists.
    
- Craft thrives in choosing the _most appropriate tool_.

**Editorial Strategy**

- Video first, transcript second
    
- Multiple entry lengths (short → long)
    
- Signed‑off edits by participants
    
- Commissioned essays (Katherine, Lynne, Rina)
