## TL;DR

Artist residencies are rare interruptions to the capitalist "grind," offering a privilege of space and time that remains baffling to those in traditional industries. However, as righteously pointed out by a student during the residency presentation, these creative havens often rest upon colonial foundations of land dispossession and aristocratic patronage. But also righteously pointed by the presenting faculty member that, it can be a driving force for decolonization narrative through intentional, ancestral practice.

## Context: Why I'm writing about residency and why it matters?

The 
The conversation around the **AI Commons** often focuses on digital data sovereignty, but the "commons" is also physical. Residencies are the terrestrial version of a common-pool resource—shared spaces where knowledge is produced outside of market logic. In an era of rapid AI extraction, the residency offers "slow time" to integrate **ancestral knowledge through action**, ensuring that the "outputs" of our culture are rooted in land and lineage rather than just algorithmic efficiency.

---

## My take

### 1. The Privilege of the "Productive Pause"

To an outsider, the concept of a residency is an anomaly. In most sectors, "work" is defined by clear deliverables and billable hours. A residency, however, is a **surprising phenomenon** because it subsidizes the _state of being_ over the _act of producing_.

Whether it is a boat residency in the Arctic or a studio in Paris at the **Cité internationale des arts**, these spaces offer a break from the "regular routine." They allow for what Elinor Ostrom identifies as the governance of a shared resource—in this case, the resource of **unstructured time**. It is a profound privilege to be "self-directed," as seen in the **BANFF Centre**, where Yann Martel birthed _Life of Pi_. This "productive pause" is a radical reclamation of human agency in a world that demands constant, visible output.

### 2. The Colonial Ghost in the Studio

While residencies feel like neutral spaces of creativity, they carry a "shadow" of historical baggage. Modern residencies are the descendants of **aristocratic patronage** and the **19th-century "Grand Tour,"** where elite artists traveled to "capture" (and thereby culturally claim) distant lands.

- **Extraction and Landscapes:** Many iconic Canadian sites, like **Banff**, are located in National Parks—territories often created through the displacement of Indigenous peoples (such as the Stoney Nakoda) to create a "pristine" wilderness for European-style leisure.
    
- **Corporate Entanglement:** We must also reconcile the "oil and gas money" that historically funded institutions in Alberta, highlighting how creative freedom is often a byproduct of extractive industries.
    
- **Decolonizing the Practice:** As explored in Mihyun Maria Kim’s OCAD thesis, **_The Measure of Han_**, artists are now using these spaces to address "untranslatable Korean affect" and intergenerational trauma. By bringing "inherited objects" and "daily activations" into the residency, artists can transform a historically colonial structure into a site for **ancestral recovery**, moving from "capturing" the land to "listening" to it.
