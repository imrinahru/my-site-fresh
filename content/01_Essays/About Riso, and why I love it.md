Have you heard of the **Risograph**?

When I first encountered the term, I confused it with _lithograph_. Isn't the prefix _litho-_ stands for stone? indeed lithography is a venerable printmaking technique using stone surfaces that dates back to the 18th century. It sounds heavy, academic, and ancient.

"Risograph" sounds just as serious, but the reality is much more charming. "Riso" is simply the name of the small Japanese company, Riso Kagaku Corporation, that invented the machine. Riso simply means "high ambition, dream" in Japanese. Originally designed as a high-volume photocopier for schools and offices, it has since been "reclaimed" by artists and zine-makers who fell in love with its unique aesthetic.

### The Charm of the Temperamental Machine

I can’t quite remember the exact moment I was drawn to it, but I’ve realized I feel far more at peace sitting in a room full of Riso prints, with a riso machine that constantly squeaks and trembles, than I ever do staring at things that only live on screens.

The source of this enjoyment might be materiality. It’s in the mechanical printing marks, the texture of the uncoated paper, and the vibrant, semi-transparent inks. It’s even in the "labor" of it, as the machines require constant cleaning, ink-drum swapping, and maintenance. But that effort results in a physical work that you can hold and post on any surface in the real world.

### The Magic of the Process

One of the most addictive parts of Riso is when all the colors get printed, registered beautifully on a fresh paper. No matter how many time I do it, the moment still feels so magical. Because the ink is translucent and colors are layered one by one, you can never fully predict how a print will look until it slides out of the machine. This process opened my world to the logic of CMYK, common printing techniques, and the only moment I appreciated Illustrator and Photoshop, as well as the vibrant underground scene of zines and self-published books.

I recently found inspiration in how a colleague at the studio approaches her work. For her, the Riso is the perfect bridge because she can move fluidly between the **digital and the analogue**:

1. She creates original artwork using pen or pencil (Analogue).
2. She scans it, then lays it out and retouches it in Photoshop (Digital).
3. She sends it back to the Riso to be birthed into the world (Analogue).

It goes from paper, to pixels, and back to paper. There is a beautiful intentionality in that loop. I’ve even seen her deliberately use **scrap papers**—sheets that others have already printed on—to see what "glitches" or unexpected textures might emerge. That level of play is something you just don't get with a standard inkjet printer.

### A Community of Machines and Makers

Riso is rarely a solitary hobby; it’s a **community**. You see it in the way studios treat their equipment. In our studio, the machines aren't just hardware; they have personalities and names—**Gail and Doris**. When you name a machine, you acknowledge its quirks and its "moods."

### The Future: Bridging the Digital and the Material

If I want to bridge the digital world with the Risograph, there are two specific areas I’m itching to explore:

- **Translating Digital Artifacts:** I want to create content that highlights the tension between the pixel and the page. This includes experimenting with **Moiré patterns** and **Pixel Art**. By taking pixelated color schemes and changing the pigment size or layer order, I can bring the "untouchable" digital aesthetic back into the physical realm. It makes us remember the digital structures we usually take for granted.
- **Code-Based Printing:** I want to contribute to and expand **p5.riso**. By using code to generate art specifically for the Risograph, I can link the creative coding community with the printmaking community, finding new ways to automate beauty while keeping the "soul" of the ink.

Riso reminds me that art is meant to be felt, held, and shared. It’s a reminder that even in a digital age, there is nothing quite like the smell of fresh ink on a scrap of paper.
