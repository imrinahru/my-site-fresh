Serving as a TA for a business and social innovation course within an art and design school gave me some thoughts on the difficulty of bridging the two domains and I’d like to take some time to note down what went right and what went wrong in my opinion.

## Students' Motivation and Intention

Students were not resistant to business concepts, rather, they were eager to learn about business concepts and use that knowledge to their advantage as they step into the business world in 1~3 years. What strikes me is how much these young adults sought after knowledge about "business", but the idea sits in them only vaguely, analogous to how some people wanting to visit "Paris", but the idea sits in them only vaguely. 

On the one side, the narrative of how "business" is indispensable to their success (or avoidance of failure) seemed to have planted a deep seed in their mind. They absorbed the capitalist ethos with their sensitivity more than anyone else. On the other side, the business education is lacking in their surroundings, which motivated them to take this course that has two keywords they sought after: "business" and "innovation".

So the students thought the course will prepare them to better step into the business world, make them more marketable and one even said bluntly that they want to learn how "to be successful and rich".

But the course provider was not looking to do that, but exactly proposing the opposite.


## Business as a Language, Not a Discipline


 wary of _what business represents_. For many, “business” was associated with extraction, compromise, or creative dilution. This shaped the affective environment of learning more than the content itself.

The role of the TA, then, was not to persuade, but to **reframe**.

One of the most effective shifts I observed was treating business frameworks not as prescriptions, but as **shared vocabularies**.

Tools like the Business Model Canvas, SWOT, or STEEPLE became useful only once students understood that:

- These are _descriptive tools_, not moral positions
    
- They help articulate assumptions that already exist in creative work
    
- They can be bent, hybridized, or rejected once understood

## Leadership, Anxiety, and Permission to Slow Down

A recurring theme across tutorials was anxiety—about leadership, responsibility, and legitimacy.

Many students associated leadership with dominance or authority, rather than coordination or care. Introducing leadership as _relational_ rather than positional helped dissolve some of this tension. Concepts like:

- Psychological safety
    
- Cognitive diversity
    
- Followership
    
- Productive conflict
    

resonated strongly when paired with students’ lived experiences of group work, collectives, and peer critique.

One instructor remark—“go slow to go far”—proved especially important. In a culture that often valorizes speed, iteration, and output, **slowness became a pedagogical intervention**.

---

## Social Enterprise as a Spectrum, Not a Category

Case studies such as Eva’s Print Shop, StopGap, or Free Geek Toronto were most effective when presented not as ideal models, but as **situated responses**.

Students benefited from understanding that:

- All organizations operate within social systems
    
- Social enterprise is defined by _intentional prioritization_, not moral purity
    
- Trade-offs are inevitable and visible
    

This reframing helped move discussion away from guilt or idealism and toward **structural thinking**—particularly when paired with the triple bottom line and later helix models.

The progression from the Triple Helix (government–industry–research), to the Quadruple Helix (civil society), and ultimately the Quintuple Helix (environment), offered students a way to situate their creative practice within broader systemic relationships—without forcing them into predefined roles.

---

## What This TA Experience Clarified for Me

Being a TA in this context reinforced several convictions about teaching, learning, and design education:

- Facilitation is more powerful than explanation
    
- Business education in art schools must begin with values, not metrics
    
- Frameworks work best when treated as provisional scaffolding
    
- Learning emerges through repetition, dialogue, and permission to question
    

Most importantly, I learned that **art students are already system thinkers**. They simply arrive through intuition, material practice, and affect rather than abstraction. The task of business education, and of the TA within it, is to meet them there—and translate, not override, their ways of knowing.
