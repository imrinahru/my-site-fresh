## Heat set textured surfaces

• Thermal heat setting with wool works with the fact that wool shrinks, using the same principles as in felt making, but this time using existing woven or knitted fabrics.
• Thermal heat setting with polyester works because it is a thermoplastic material that responds to heat. When heated to 200-240 C/392-464 F) for 20-30 minutes, polyester will be permanently set to whatever 3-dimensional texture is imposed upon it.

### History 

In Greece the pleated chiton [khiton] garment was made of wool, linen and
sometimes cotton. The pieces of material were set into pleats, soaked in a thin starch solution, twisted and tied at the ends, then left in the sun to dry. This gave a greater permanence to the pleating.

## Thermal Heat Setting Fabrics

Wool fabrics can be permanently transformed through applying resist methods (such as binding, stitching and clamping) and then washing in hot water or steaming in the pressure cooker to shrink the wool fibres into new forms and shapes. Woven, knitted or felted animal wool and hair fibres are suited to this process.

Polyester fabrics can be manipulated into interesting 3-dimensional forms and textures using the steaming method or a heat press. 

#### Materials

- Handmade prefelt/finished felt, commercial wool felt, 100% wool knit or woven fabrics (wool, cashmere, mohair, angora), used knit or woven wool clothing
- Woven polyester fabrics
- Yarn or sewing thread (strong thread that can withstand pulling and tying) or strong rubber bands
    - _Thin nylon cord, some polyester sewing thread and dental floss all work well_
- Small heat resistant objects such as glass marbles, stones, beans, wood, corks, pennies, washers, nails, etc.
- Sharp scissors
- Sewing needles
- Clamps, wooden boards, office clips
- Large stock pot & stove top (fulling)
- Stove top pressure cooker (steaming)
- Brown kraft paper
- Tongs
- Tray
- Clean towels

### Preparing the fabrics

#### Wool Fabrics - Design Options

- **Bind objects:** Various small, heat resistant objects can be tied into the fabric and then bound with rubber bands or strong thread/string.
- **Shibori binding:** Consider shibori techniques like kanoko (small dots), kumo (spiderweb - large points) and bai (shell - small points) binding.
- **Shibori stitching:** Fabrics can be stitched by hand, by machine with a long stitch length, or using the smocking machine in the studio. Stitch with strong thread that can withstand high tension, gather and bind off tightly.
- **Shibori folding:** in the itajime style, work with accordion pleats to start - then fold, bind or clamp the fabric to secure it for heat setting. Pleats can be regular or varied in size for interesting effects.
- **Slashing:** To create openings in the fabric, cut slits, holes and slashes over the entire surface or along an edge. The openings will have finished edges once the fabric is fulled. This can create lacey surfaces or provide opportunity for ribbons or other fabrics to be woven through the cloth.

#### Polyester Fabrics - Design Options

### Fulling Wool - Boiling Method

1. Bring a large pot of water to 90˚C, making sure that there is enough to cover the fabric.
2. Add the prepared textiles to the hot water.
3. Stir to make sure the materials are well distributed in the pot and not sitting on the bottom.
4. Allow just to reach a low boil, then turn down to a simmer. Simmer for 30 minutes, stirring occasionally.
5. Carefully lift the fabric out of the pot, letting it drip back into the pot for a minute.
6. With heat resistant gloves, squeeze out excess water and lay out on a rack to cool.
7. Once fabric is dry, remove binding and objects.
8. Don’t use iron to press – allow to air dry.

### Fulling Wool and Heat Setting Polyester - Stove Top Pressure Cooker

1. Wrap prepared fabric in brown paper first - each piece should be its own compact parcel.
2. To use the stove top pressure cooker, sign out from the studio office is required. Always read the instruction sheet before using the equipment.
3. Remove instruction sheet, paper circles and metal base plate from the pressure cooker. 
4. Fill the base of the pressure cooker with 2-3" of water - do not exceed the height of the wire rack at the bottom.
5. Replace the metal base plate and stack 7 paper circles on top. (These papers can be dried and resued - do not throw away!)
6. Set the pressure cooker on the largest ring on the stove. Stack all fabric parcels inside the cooker, ensuring that nothing touches the sides and that the lid can easily close.
7. Close the lid by matching up the open/close arrow and twisting the lid.
8. Set stove to high heat and wait for the pressure to build inside the cooker. The pressure indicator will start to splutter; when this happens, gently lift the indicator so it remains raised. **Wear black latex gloves inside the large black heatproof gloves when touching the hot pressure cooker.** 
9. When the pressure gauge reaches 10, turn the stove down to 4. This can take 15-25 minutes.
10. The cooker lid will start to rattle when it has reached a boil - this is when you start timing the steaming process. Set a timer for 30 minutes.
11. Monitor the pressure cooker in the 30 minute steaming time to ensure the pressure level remains below 15. If the pressure is too high, release steam by gently pushing the metal pressure indicator to one side and/or turn down heat on the stove. If the pressure is dangerously high, remove from the heat to reduce pressure quickly.
12. After 30 minutes of steaming, turn off the heat and remove the cooker from the hot ring. Wait for the pressure to reduce to 0 before opening the lid. The cooling process should be gradual, but if it is taking too long it can be sped up by gently pushing the metal pressure indicator to one side.
13. When the pressure gauge is at 0, use the heat proof gloves to open the lid and remove the samples into a tray with tongs. Unwrap the fabrics when cool and allow to dry with the binding in tact for maximum textural effects.
