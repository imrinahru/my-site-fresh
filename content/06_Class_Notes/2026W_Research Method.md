# Core Research Concepts

> **Research = “poking with intent.”**

Research is a **structured way of generating knowledge**.
Research heavily inherits European positivist pov, where researcher is separated from the rest (subjects), starts from a hypothesis that needs to be validated by yes or no.

### Layers of Research

1. **Ontology / Philosophy**
    
    - What counts as knowledge?
        
    - What kinds of knowledge matter?
        
2. **Methodology**
    
    - The theoretical approach guiding the research.
        
3. **Methods**
    
    - The structured way data is gathered.
        
4. **Techniques**
    
    - Specific tools used to execute methods.
        

Example:

| Level       | Example                                                    |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Methodology | Constructivist / qualitative, or an established discipline |
| Method      | Interview                                                  |
| Technique   | Unstructured interview                                     |

In **interdisciplinary research**, emphasis is often placed on **method and technique**, since methodologies may come from different disciplines.

In the context of digital futures, an interdisciplinary program, discipline can be fluid, and hence the methodology. It also aligns with change, transformation. 

So it's possible to only specify method and technique while not delimiting a methodology.

However, it's still important to understand how method is stacked above a ground theory, what is used, what is not used, and what is adapted.

From higher level to lower level:

| Ontology                      | Epistemology                     | Theoretical Knowledge                      | Methodology                                                                                   | Methods                                                                    |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What exist? What facts count? | How do we know? Why is it valid? | What approach can we use to get knowledge? | What procedure (theory+philosophy)? Is there an established one (a domain)? like anthropology | What approach/tools/sources should be used, what data should be collected? |
It represents a worldview, what do we value?

Validation vs. Understanding vs. Action
- Usability studies: replication, prediction, reduction, validation
- Empathic tools: understanding, participatory
- Action research: applied research. within an explicit change agenda

## Cresswell's Four Worldviews

In terms of methodology and method used: 

Postpositivist: data science, hard science, valid, reproducible, researcher bias is considered

Constructivist: HCI, meaning making, context dependent, qualitative

Transformative: social justice, feminist, decolonial, participatory

Pragmatism: usability testing, application


Transformative and pragmatism are newer addition, which digital futures often identify with.

# Design Research Approaches

Design research often involves **co-creation with participants**.

Common approaches include:

- **Participatory Design**
    
    - Tools such as the **Convivial Toolbox**

# Attribution and AI in Research

Using AI in academic work requires **clear attribution**.

Required disclosure includes:

- **Which AI tool was used**
    
- **Where it was used in the process**
    
- **How it influenced writing or ideation**
    
- **Prompts or inputs provided**
    
- **How AI output was integrated into the final work**
    

Example documentation:

- Original writing
    
- AI-assisted revision
    

However, the **creation process is rarely linear**, so documenting **iterative back-and-forth interactions** may be more accurate.

Important note:

- If a course **prohibits AI use**, using it may constitute **academic misconduct**.

# Design Thinking

Definition:

Design thinking combines:

- **Empathy** for users and context
    
- **Creativity** for generating solutions
    
- **Rational analysis** for evaluating solutions.
    

Classic model:

**Divergence + Convergence** (Tim Brown, 2009)

### Double Diamond Model

1. Discover
    
2. Define
    
3. Develop
    
4. Deliver
    

Key characteristics:

- iterative
    
- non-linear
    
- exploratory.
    

---

# Critiques of Design Thinking

A common criticism:

- Design thinking often focuses **only on expanding options**, not on deeper systemic change.
    

Important considerations:

- **Unintended consequences**
    
- **Interlocking systems**
    
- **Cascading effects**
    
- **Diverse perspectives and inclusion**
    

Innovation is defined as:

> **A successful idea implemented in practice.**

However, we must ask:

- Successful **for whom?**
    
- According to **whose standards?**
    

Sometimes **care and sustained struggle** are more valuable than spectacular innovation.

# Collective Genius (Collaborative Innovation)

Successful collaborative innovation requires:

### 1. Willingness & Community

Key components:

- **Purpose**
    
    - Why do we exist?
        
- **Shared values**
    
    - What do we collectively believe is important?
        
- **Rules of engagement**
    
    - How people are treated
        
    - Process and hierarchy
        
    - Communication norms.
        

Managing toxicity is critical — both **mundane and severe forms**.

---

### 2. Capabilities

Effective teams develop:

- **Idea conflict**
    
    - Productive disagreement
        
- **Agility**
    
    - Continuous experimentation
        
- **Resolution**
    
    - Integrating opposing ideas.
        

Resolution is often **the most difficult stage**, especially when ideas are fundamentally incompatible.

# Creative Problem-Solving Profiles (Basadur Model)

People approach innovation differently.

Two axes:

### Knowledge Acquisition

How people prefer to learn:

- **Direct experience**
    
- **Abstract thinking**
    

### Knowledge Use

How people apply knowledge:

- **Evaluation**
    
    - critique and analysis
        
- **Ideation**
    
    - generating new possibilities.
        

Four creative roles:

1. **Generator**
    
2. **Conceptualizer**
    
3. **Optimizer**
    
4. **Actioner**
    

These roles form **a cycle of innovation**.

Tensions often occur between roles positioned **opposite each other in the cycle**.

# Research Ethics

Ethical research is **a habit, not a checklist**.

Researchers must consider:

- **Research fatigue**
    
- **Extraction from communities**
    
- **Power imbalances**
    

Key ethical test:

Research should not proceed if:

- The **risk exceeds the benefit**
    
- The **community does not benefit**
    
- The **knowledge generated has little value**
    

Even **public observation requires ethical awareness**, especially when discussing unpublished or sensitive information.

# Historical Ethical Failures

Examples discussed:

- **Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972)**
    
- **Stanford Prison Experiment (1971)**
    
- **CDC Vaccine Study in Africa (2026)**
    

These cases illustrate the need for **strong research governance**.

# Equity-Centered Research (Chicago Beyond, 2019)

Important principles:

- **Access**
    
    - representation in research
        
- **Information**
    
    - transparency of research process
        
- **Validity**
    
    - community knowledge is legitimate expertise
        
- **Ownership**
    
    - who owns the knowledge generated
        
- **Value**
    
    - who benefits and at what cost
        
- **Accountability**
    
- **Authorship**
    
    - who receives credit.

# Tri-Council Ethics Framework (Canada)

Public research institutions follow **TCPS2** guidelines.

Main agencies:

- **NSERC** — Natural sciences and engineering
    
- **SSHRC** — Social sciences and humanities
    
- **CIHR** — Health research
    

Ethical review:

- Conducted by a **Research Ethics Board (REB)**.
    

---

# Informed Consent

Participants must:

- Understand the study
    
- Be able to **explain the study back to the researcher**
    

Consent must be:

- **Ongoing**
    
- **Documented**
    

Researchers must clearly explain:

- Time commitment
    
- Procedures
    
- Risks and benefits.
